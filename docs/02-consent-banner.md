## 2. Consent Banner

### 2.1 Definition of valid consent

**Requirement:** Consent must be freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous, expressed through a clear affirmative action (an unambiguous statement or positive act).

**Source:** Art. 4(11) GDPR; Recital 32 GDPR.

**Notes:** Silence, pre-ticked checkboxes and inactivity do NOT constitute consent. Consent must relate to one or more specific purposes (it cannot be generic). If consent is requested electronically, the request must be "clear, concise and not unnecessarily disruptive to the use of the service for which it is provided" (Recital 32 GDPR).

---

### 2.2 Prior blocking of non-essential cookies

**Requirement:** No cookies or tracking technologies other than strictly necessary (technical) ones shall be placed on the user's device before the user has given consent.

**Source:** Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC.

**Notes:** Technical cookies (strictly necessary to provide a service explicitly requested by the user) are exempt. The blocking must be technically enforced, not merely declared. National DPAs may have additional specific requirements on prior blocking implementation.

---

### 2.3 Exemptions from consent

**Requirement:** Only the following are exempt from consent: (a) technical storage for the sole purpose of transmitting a communication over a network, and (b) storage strictly necessary to provide a service explicitly requested by the user.

**Source:** Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC.

**Notes:** "Strictly necessary" must be interpreted restrictively. Language preference cookies, shopping cart cookies, and session authentication cookies qualify; basic analytics cookies do NOT (except under specific conditions, see 2.19).

---

### 2.4 Mandatory banner components (first layer)

**Requirement:** The consent banner must contain:
1. A notice that closing the banner via the X button maintains the default settings (no tracking)
2. A concise disclosure indicating: (a) the site uses technical cookies, (b) it may use profiling cookies or other tracking technologies subject to consent, (c) the specific purposes of non-technical cookies (e.g. behavioural advertising, service personalisation, behaviour analysis)
3. A link to the extended cookie policy (second layer), accessible with a single click
4. A command to accept all cookies
5. A link to the granular selection area (customisation by category and/or individual third party)
6. An X button in the upper right corner to close the banner

**Source:** EDPB Guidelines 05/2020 on consent (transparency and layered approach); Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC; Recital 32 GDPR.

**Notes:** The banner must have adequate dimensions to create a perceptible interruption in the page content experience. Dimensions must be evaluated for different device types (mobile, tablet, desktop) and must avoid the risk of unintended or unconscious user actions. National DPAs may have additional specific requirements on banner components.

---

### 2.5 "Accept all" button

**Requirement:** The banner must include a command allowing the user to accept all cookies and tracking technologies.

**Source:** EDPB Guidelines 05/2020 on consent (clear affirmative action); Recital 32 GDPR.

**Notes:** The action must be exclusively an opt-in (a positive action aimed at granting consent), not an opt-out.

---

### 2.6 Rejection mechanism: "Reject all" button

**Requirement:** The banner must include a mechanism to reject all non-essential cookies. The user must be able to refuse consent with the same ease as granting it.

**Source:** EDPB Guidelines 05/2020, par. 86; EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on Deceptive Design Patterns; Art. 7(3) GDPR.

**Notes:** An explicit "Reject all" button with equal visual prominence to the "Accept all" button is necessary to ensure parity between acceptance and refusal. The EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 specifically address the "Hindering" pattern, where refusing consent is made harder than accepting it.

---

### 2.7 Visual parity of buttons

**Requirement:** All banner commands must have equal visual prominence: same dimensions, same emphasis, same colours. No button shall be visually dominant over the others.

**Source:** EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on Deceptive Design Patterns ("Stirring" pattern); EDPB Guidelines 05/2020.

**Notes:** It is prohibited to use dark patterns such as coloured buttons for "Accept" and faded links for "Reject". Visual parity extends explicitly to the X close button, which must not be hidden or made less visible than the "Accept" button. See EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on deceptive design patterns.

---

### 2.8 Granular selection area (customisation)

**Requirement:** The banner must contain a link to an analytical selection area where the user can choose, including individually, the functionalities, third parties and cookies — also grouped by homogeneous categories — that they wish to accept.

**Source:** EDPB Guidelines 05/2020, par. 56 and 60 (granularity of consent); Art. 4(11) GDPR.

**Notes:** Categories must distinguish at least between technical, analytics and profiling cookies. The ability to select by individual third party is a distinct requirement from category-only selection. The list of third parties must be kept constantly up to date. National DPAs may have additional specific requirements on granular selection implementation.

---

### 2.9 Toggles pre-set to refusal (privacy by default)

**Requirement:** In the customisation area, all consent options must be initially pre-set to refusal (denial). Only technical/necessary cookies may be pre-activated and non-deactivatable.

**Source:** Recital 32 GDPR; CJEU C-673/17 (Planet49); EDPB Guidelines 05/2020.

**Notes:** Pre-ticked checkboxes or pre-activated toggles set to "accept" do not constitute valid consent.

---

### 2.10 Prohibition of scroll and swiping as consent

**Requirement:** Scrolling, swiping and any similar user activity on the page cannot under any circumstances constitute valid consent.

**Source:** EDPB Guidelines 05/2020, par. 86 (Example 16).

**Notes:** The EDPB is categorical: scrolling and swiping "will not under any circumstances satisfy the requirement of a clear and affirmative action" for three reasons: (a) it is indistinguishable from normal browsing, (b) unambiguous consent cannot be determined, (c) withdrawal cannot be made equally easy (violation of Art. 7(3)). It is recommended not to rely on scrolling as a form of consent.

---

### 2.11 Prohibition of cookie walls

**Requirement:** It is prohibited to condition access to the website on acceptance of cookies ("take it or leave it" mechanism).

**Source:** EDPB Guidelines 05/2020, par. 38-41.

**Notes:** The only exception is when the controller offers access to equivalent content or service without requiring consent, in compliance with Art. 5(1)(a) GDPR. In practice: if the user refuses cookies, they must still be able to access the site and its main content. The EDPB further clarifies that a controller CANNOT justify a cookie wall by arguing that the user can turn to a competitor: the freedom of consent cannot depend on the practices of third parties (EDPB 05/2020, par. 40).

**Exception for specific services:** If a specific feature of the site technically requires a non-essential cookie to function (e.g. embedded YouTube video), the controller may condition access to that specific feature on consent, provided that: (a) the rest of the site remains accessible, (b) a substitute notice is displayed explaining why the content is unavailable and how to activate it.

---

### 2.12 Withdrawal of consent

**Requirement:** The user must be able to modify their cookie choices at any time, in a simple, immediate and intuitive manner. Withdrawal must be as easy as giving consent.

**Source:** Art. 7(3) GDPR.

**Notes:** If consent is obtained with a single click, withdrawal must require the same level of simplicity. The user must be informed of the right to withdraw before giving consent. Subsequent choices override previous ones.

---

### 2.13 "Review your choices" link in footer

**Requirement:** The website must contain a permanent link in the footer of every page, with a label such as "review your cookie choices" or similar, allowing the user to reopen the consent management panel.

**Source:** Art. 7(3) GDPR (ease of withdrawal); EDPB Guidelines 05/2020.

**Notes:** It is also recommended to display a graphical sign, icon, or technical solution visible on every page to indicate the current consent status. National DPAs may have additional specific requirements on the placement and labelling of this link.

---

### 2.14 Re-prompting / Re-presentation of the banner

**Requirement:** The banner may be re-presented only in three cases:
1. The processing conditions have changed significantly (e.g. new third parties, new purposes)
2. The controller cannot verify whether the consent preference cookie is still present (the user has deleted it)
3. At least 6 months have elapsed since the last presentation of the banner

**Source:** Art. 7(1) GDPR (demonstrability of consent); EDPB Guidelines 05/2020.

**Notes:** Excessive re-presentation of the banner is considered an autonomous ground of unlawfulness: "excessive re-prompting when the user has previously refused appears likely to undermine their freedom" by inducing consent for the sole purpose of continuing navigation (anti-consent-fatigue principle). The system must prevent re-prompting before 6 months if the user has expressed a choice. National DPAs may have additional specific requirements on re-prompting intervals.

---

### 2.15 Demonstrability of consent

**Requirement:** The controller must be able to demonstrate that the data subject has given consent to the processing of their personal data.

**Source:** Art. 7(1) GDPR; Recital 42 GDPR.

**Notes:** A consent recording/audit system is required. The record must include at least: anonymous identifier, timestamp, event type (accept/refuse/customise), choices made, consent version. The IP address must be anonymised in the log to prevent the log itself from becoming an unnecessary processing operation.

---

### 2.16 Specific and granular consent

**Requirement:** Consent must be specific to each purpose. The controller must provide a separate opt-in for each processing purpose. It is not lawful to bundle different purposes under a single consent where it is appropriate to separate them.

**Source:** Art. 4(11) GDPR; Recital 43 GDPR; EDPB Guidelines 05/2020, par. 56 and 60.

**Notes:** Example: consent for analytics cookies and consent for marketing/profiling cookies must be separate, not combined into a single "non-necessary" toggle. Recital 43 specifies that consent is presumed not to be freely given if it does not allow separate consent to be given for distinct processing operations, where this would be appropriate in the individual case.

---

### 2.17 Freely given consent and absence of conditioning

**Requirement:** Consent is not valid if the provision of a service is conditional on consent to processing that is not necessary for the performance of that service.

**Source:** Art. 7(4) GDPR; Recital 42-43 GDPR; EDPB Guidelines 05/2020, par. 38-41.

**Notes:** The user must have a genuine and free choice. Refusal of consent must not result in any detriment to the user in accessing the service. Consent is not free if the data subject cannot refuse or withdraw it "without detriment" (Recital 42). In relationships with significant power imbalance (e.g. employee-employer, citizen-public authority), consent is presumed not to be free (Recital 43).

---

### 2.18 Partial nullity of non-compliant clauses

**Requirement:** Any part of a consent declaration that constitutes a violation of the GDPR is not binding.

**Source:** Art. 7(2), last sentence, GDPR.

**Notes:** If a consent request contains non-compliant clauses (e.g. mandatory consent for non-necessary purposes), such clauses are void without invalidating the entire consent. The controller must verify that every element of the consent request is compliant.

---

### 2.19 Analytics cookies exempt from consent (conditions)

**Requirement:** Analytics cookies may be treated as equivalent to technical cookies (exempt from consent) ONLY if all the following conditions are met:
1. IP address masked: at least the last octet for IPv4 (uncertainty of 1/256, corresponding to approximately 0.4%), equivalent procedures for IPv6
2. Limited to a single site/app (no cross-site tracking)
3. Only aggregate statistics
4. Third parties must not combine the masked data with other datasets
5. Third parties must not transmit the data to additional third parties
6. Conditions 1-5 must be guaranteed by contractual obligations between the controller and the third-party analytics provider (DPA under Art. 28 GDPR or specific contractual clauses)

**Source:** Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC; EDPB Guidelines 05/2020 (purpose limitation and data minimisation as conditions for exemption).

**Notes:** If even one condition is not met, analytics cookies require consent like any other non-technical cookie. The objective is to prevent "singling out" (direct identification of the data subject). Exception: analysis of multiple domains, websites or apps attributable to the same publisher or business group is permitted, provided that the statistical processing is conducted by the controller itself and does not take on the characteristics of processing for commercial decisions. National DPAs may have additional specific requirements on the conditions for analytics cookie exemptions.

---

### 2.20 Information before consent

**Requirement:** Before giving consent, the user must be informed of: (a) the identity of the controller, (b) the purposes of the processing, (c) the right to withdraw consent.

**Source:** Art. 7(2)-(3) GDPR; Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive; Recital 42 GDPR.

**Notes:** The consent request must be presented in a manner clearly distinguishable from other information, in an intelligible and easily accessible form, using clear and plain language. The request must not be "unnecessarily disruptive to the use of the service for which it is provided" (Recital 32 GDPR).

---

### 2.21 Clear affirmative action

**Requirement:** Consent must be expressed through a clear affirmative action (e.g. clicking a button, ticking a checkbox). The following are not valid: silence, inactivity, pre-ticked checkboxes, continued browsing.

**Source:** Recital 32 GDPR; Art. 4(11) GDPR; CJEU C-673/17 (Planet49); EDPB Guidelines 05/2020.

**Notes:** The action must be "exclusively aimed at expressing consent (opt-in)".

---

### 2.22 Storage of user choices

**Requirement:** User choices must be stored via a dedicated technical cookie or equivalent technology, to avoid re-presentation of the banner on every visit.

**Source:** Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC (technical cookie exemption); EDPB Guidelines 05/2020.

**Notes:** The choice-storage cookie is classified as a technical cookie (strictly necessary) and does not require consent. The cookie should be configured with appropriate security attributes: `Secure` (transmission only over HTTPS), `SameSite=Lax` or `SameSite=Strict` (CSRF protection), and a duration consistent with the 6-month re-prompt period (see 2.14). The `HttpOnly` attribute is recommended if the cookie does not need to be read by client-side JavaScript; if the implementation requires client-side reading, the rationale should be documented. National DPAs may have additional specific requirements on consent storage duration.

---

### 2.23 Accessibility of the banner

**Requirement:** The banner and all consent mechanisms must comply with accessibility requirements, usable via assistive technologies without discrimination.

**Source:** Directive (EU) 2019/882 (European Accessibility Act), applicable from 28 June 2025.

**Notes:** Practical requirements: keyboard navigation (Tab, Enter, Escape), focus trap in modal, ARIA attributes, screen reader support, respect for reduced motion preferences. Information may also be provided through multiple channels and modalities (multichannel): video channels, informational pop-ups, voice interactions, virtual assistants, chatbots, telephone. From 28 June 2025, the European Accessibility Act imposes accessibility requirements conforming to standard EN 301 549 v3.2.1 (harmonised with WCAG 2.1 level AA) for all digital services offered to consumers — including consent banners.

---

### 2.24 Third-party embedded content

**Requirement:** Embedded content that installs third-party cookies (e.g. YouTube iframes, social widgets, maps) must be preventively blocked until the user's consent is obtained. In the absence of consent, a notice must be displayed explaining the need for consent and offering a link to modify preferences.

**Source:** Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC.

**Notes:** For YouTube videos, using the `youtube-nocookie.com` domain reduces but does not entirely eliminate cookies. The safest solution is blocking the iframe until consent is given. The substitute notice must contain a direct link to the preference management panel.

---

### 2.25 Google Consent Mode v2

**Requirement:** If using Google Consent Mode v2, all consent signals must be set to "denied" by default before loading any Google tag. The signals to manage are: `ad_storage`, `ad_user_data`, `ad_personalization`, `analytics_storage`.

**Source:** Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC (prior blocking as a principle); Art. 6(1)(a) GDPR.

**Notes:** Google Consent Mode is a technical tool provided by Google, not a regulatory requirement in itself. GDPR compliance must be ensured regardless of whether Consent Mode is used. The "denied" default value is consistent with the principle of prior blocking under the ePrivacy Directive.

---

### 2.26 Consent versioning

**Requirement:** In case of significant changes to processing conditions, previously collected consent is no longer valid and must be collected again.

**Source:** Art. 7(1) GDPR.

**Notes:** It is good practice to implement a consent versioning system (e.g. `CONSENT_VERSION`) that, upon update, automatically invalidates previous choices and re-presents the banner.

---

### 2.27 Fingerprinting and device recognition

**Requirement:** Browser/device fingerprinting and any passive identification technique (canvas fingerprinting, audio fingerprinting, font enumeration, WebGL fingerprinting) are treated as equivalent to non-technical cookies and require the user's prior consent.

**Source:** Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC; EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 on Technical Scope of Art. 5(3).

**Notes:** Fingerprinting is expressly included among "other tracking technologies" subject to the same rules as cookies. Unlike cookies, fingerprinting does not store information on the device but reads it — however Art. 5(3) ePrivacy covers both the storage of and access to information already present on the terminal device. Prior blocking applies in full.

---

### 2.28 Server-side tracking and tagging

**Requirement:** Server-side tracking techniques (e.g. server-side tagging for GA4, Meta Conversions API, server-side pixels) do not exempt from consent. If the tracking serves purposes that are not strictly technical (analytics, marketing, profiling), consent is still required regardless of the technical architecture.

**Source:** Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC (broad interpretation); Art. 6(1)(a) GDPR; EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 on Technical Scope of Art. 5(3).

**Notes:** Server-side tagging moves processing from the browser to the server, but does not change the nature of the processing. If the data collected (e.g. navigation event, conversion) is sent to a third party for analytics or marketing purposes, consent remains required. The principle is clear: compliance is assessed based on the purpose of the processing, not the technique used.

---

### 2.29 Pay-or-consent model

**Requirement:** The "pay or consent" model (access to the website conditional on accepting cookies or paying a subscription) is permissible only under very restrictive conditions. The paid alternative must be genuine, reasonable and non-punitive.

**Source:** EDPB Guidelines 05/2020, par. 38-41 (freedom of consent); EDPB Opinion 08/2024 on "consent or pay" models.

**Notes:** The EDPB has expressed a critical position on pay-or-consent models of large platforms (Opinion 08/2024 on Meta). Key points: (a) consent is not free if the only alternative is a payment, unless the paid service is genuinely equivalent and the price proportionate; (b) the controller must still offer an equivalent alternative without tracking for advertising purposes; (c) small publishers have more room for manoeuvre compared to large platforms, given the different power imbalance. For most standard websites, the pay-or-consent model is discouraged due to the high risk of challenge.

---

### 2.30 Session recording, heatmaps and behavioural analysis

**Requirement:** Session recording tools (e.g. Hotjar, FullStory, Microsoft Clarity, LogRocket), heatmaps and behavioural analysis tools that record user interactions on the page (clicks, mouse movements, scrolling, form inputs) require the user's prior consent.

**Source:** Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC; Art. 6(1)(a) GDPR.

**Notes:** These tools access information on the user's device and collect detailed behavioural data that may constitute profiling. Specific requirements: (a) prior blocking of the script until consent is obtained, (b) clear disclosure of what is recorded, (c) automatic exclusion of sensitive fields (passwords, payment data) from recording, (d) masking of personal data entered in forms (PII masking), (e) limited and declared data retention. Microsoft Clarity, despite being free, installs cookies and collects behavioural data: it cannot be classified as "technical".

---

### 2.31 Tracking pixels and web beacons

**Requirement:** Tracking pixels (e.g. Meta Pixel/Facebook Pixel, LinkedIn Insight Tag, Twitter/X Pixel, TikTok Pixel, Pinterest Tag) and web beacons (1x1 transparent images, clear GIFs) are tracking technologies that require the user's prior consent.

**Source:** Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC; Art. 6(1)(a) GDPR.

**Notes:** Tracking pixels work by loading a code snippet that sends data to third-party servers (conversions, page views, user actions). Even if they do not install a cookie in the strict sense, they access information on the device and transmit data to third parties for marketing/profiling purposes. Requirements: (a) prior blocking of the script/tag until consent is obtained, (b) explicit declaration in the cookie policy indicating the third party, the purposes and the data transmitted, (c) possibility of withdrawing consent, (d) if using Meta Pixel with "Advanced Matching" (sending hashed email/phone), this constitutes additional processing that must be specified in the privacy notice.

---

### 2.32 Prohibition of A/B testing on the consent banner

**Requirement:** It is prohibited to conduct A/B testing or optimisation experiments on the consent banner with the objective of maximising the cookie acceptance rate. Any variation in design, text or button placement that systematically favours acceptance over refusal constitutes a deceptive design pattern.

**Source:** EDPB Guidelines 03/2022 on Deceptive Design Patterns ("Stirring" and "Skipping" patterns); Art. 4(11) GDPR (free and unambiguous consent).

**Notes:** A/B testing is legitimate for other site elements, but the consent banner is a consent collection mechanism, not a conversion element. Optimising the acceptance rate is equivalent to manipulating consent, rendering it unfree. An A/B test comparing, for example, a green "Accept" button with a red one to measure which produces more consents violates the principles of visual parity (see 2.7) and freedom of consent (see 2.17). A/B testing is permissible only if aimed at improving the clarity and comprehensibility of the banner (e.g. testing different formulations of the concise disclosure) without altering the balance between acceptance and refusal.

---

### 2.33 Multi-device consent management

**Requirement:** Consent is collected and stored per device/browser via a technical cookie (see 2.22). There is no regulatory obligation to synchronise consent across different devices of the same user. Each device/browser must be treated as an independent consent context.

**Source:** Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive (consent relates to storage/access on the user's "terminal equipment").

**Notes:** The consent cookie is local to the browser. If the user visits the site from another browser or device, the banner must be re-presented. This does not violate the prohibition on excessive re-prompting (see 2.14) because the controller cannot verify the presence of the choice cookie on a different terminal. If the site has an authentication system with user accounts, it is possible (but not mandatory) to associate consent preferences with the account rather than the local cookie — in that case, cross-device synchronisation becomes possible and the user's choices must be consistently respected across all devices. If this solution is implemented, the mechanism must be documented in the privacy notice.

---

### 2.34 Bots, crawlers and consent banner

**Requirement:** The consent banner is intended for human users. Search engine bots and crawlers (e.g. Googlebot, Bingbot) cannot give valid consent and must not be treated as data subjects for the purposes of cookie consent.

**Source:** Art. 4(1) GDPR (personal data relating to a "natural person"); Recital 14 GDPR (the GDPR does not apply to the processing of data that does not concern natural persons).

**Notes:** The banner may interfere with site indexing if it covers the main content or if JavaScript rendering prevents crawlers from accessing the page text. Practical recommendations: (a) use server-side rendering for the main content (do not depend on consent for text rendering), (b) ensure the banner does not prevent the crawler from reading the underlying content (the banner is an overlay, not a DOM block of the content), (c) do not serve different versions of the site to crawlers — cloaking is penalised by search engines and potentially problematic from a transparency perspective, (d) monitor the Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS) caused by the banner: values above 0.1 penalise Core Web Vitals (Google) and worsen user experience. Implement the banner with reserved dimensions in the layout or as a fixed overlay to minimise CLS.

---

### 2.35 Web Storage API (localStorage, sessionStorage, IndexedDB)

**Requirement:** Web Storage technologies (localStorage, sessionStorage, IndexedDB) are subject to the same rules as cookies when used for purposes that are not strictly technical. Art. 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive covers any "storage of information" or "access to information already stored in the terminal equipment" of the user, regardless of the technology used.

**Source:** Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC; EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 on Technical Scope of Art. 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive (adopted 16 October 2024).

**Notes:** localStorage and sessionStorage clearly fall within the category of active identifiers, as they require read/write operations on the user's device — exactly like cookies. The EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 definitively clarified that Art. 5(3) ePrivacy applies to any local storage technology on the device. In practice: if localStorage is used to store user preferences strictly necessary for the service (e.g. theme, language), it is equivalent to a technical cookie (exempt from consent). If it is used for analytics, tracking or profiling purposes, it requires prior consent just like a non-technical cookie.

---

### 2.36 Tracking pixels in emails

**Requirement:** Tracking pixels embedded in emails (open pixels, click tracking pixels, web beacons) fall within the scope of Art. 5(3) of the ePrivacy Directive and require the user's prior consent, unless they are strictly necessary for technical purposes.

**Source:** Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC; EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 on Technical Scope of Art. 5(3) (adopted 16 October 2024), which explicitly confirm that tracking pixels and tracking URLs fall within the scope of Art. 5(3).

**Notes:** The EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 clarify that a pixel embedded in an email automatically establishes a communication from the user's device to the sender's server — a communication that would not otherwise occur — and that the use of tracking pixels "is unlikely to escape the scope of Article 5(3)". Key principles: (a) specific consent is required for the tracking pixel, distinct from consent to receive marketing emails (which covers Art. 13 ePrivacy); (b) only pixels used exclusively for technical purposes (security, authentication) or for aggregate non-identifiable measurements are exempt; (c) each email with a tracking pixel should contain an unsubscribe/withdrawal link in the footer. For cookie policies and privacy policies: tracking pixels in emails must be declared in the privacy notice as tracking technologies, indicating the purpose, the email platform provider (e.g. Mailchimp, SendGrid, Brevo) and the data collected (opens, clicks, IP, user agent, timestamp).

---

### 2.37 Push notifications and Service Workers

**Requirement:** Web push notifications involve the collection and processing of personal data (endpoint URL, encryption keys, device token) and the storage of information on the user's device via Service Worker. Consent is required both under Art. 5(3) ePrivacy (storage/access on the terminal) and under GDPR (personal data processing).

**Source:** Art. 5(3) ePrivacy Directive 2002/58/EC; Art. 6(1)(a) GDPR; EDPB Guidelines 2/2023 on Technical Scope of Art. 5(3).

**Notes:** The browser's permission prompt ("Allow notifications") does not automatically satisfy GDPR consent requirements (freely given, specific, informed, unambiguous), as it is presented without prior disclosure. Practical requirements: (a) clear disclosure before triggering the browser's permission prompt (e.g. an informational pre-prompt explaining the purposes, data collected, and how to withdraw), (b) GDPR consent distinct from the browser's technical permission prompt, (c) ability to withdraw consent and unsubscribe from notifications as easily as subscribing (at least as easy as subscription), (d) declaration in the privacy notice of the data collected (subscription endpoint, keys), the purpose and the retention period, (e) if notifications contain tracking (tracked links, pixels), inform separately. The Service Worker registered for push notifications is classifiable as technical if strictly necessary to deliver the notification service requested by the user; it becomes subject to consent if used for additional purposes (e.g. background sync for analytics).
